\title{Decoupling What from Where: How Should a Small GUI Grounding Model Receive the Action Type?}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A GUI agent decides which action to take and where on the screen to take it; we ask how a small grounding model should receive the action type, and what the type buys it. Fine-tuning Qwen2-VL-2B with LoRA on Android in the Wild, we compare a flat baseline with five ways of supplying the type under matched data, compute, adapters, and decoding: an auxiliary loss, a hard-routed action word, an additive learned embedding, a prepended learned token, and the type written into the prompt. With five seeds, a bootstrap that clusters validation steps by episode, and seed-level paired tests, the ranking on a stream of clicks, scrolls, and type events is clear: the auxiliary loss, the additive embedding, and the prompt word each gain five to seven \hitat{0.10} points over the baseline, and hard routing and the prepended token are not distinguishable from it. The gain is a diagnosis of a preprocessing choice rather than a spatial prior. Our serializer clamps the off-screen touch point that AITW records for type events to the origin; that class degrades the flat baseline's click grounding, retraining without it lifts the baseline by four points to where no mechanism is established to beat it, and on a stream of taps and swipes none helps. For deployment, the pipeline's margin over the baseline with predicted rather than gold types is not established ($+0.016$, 95\% interval $[-0.017, +0.052]$), and a wrong type collapses every model conditioned at inference, the additive one most. The prepended token does not help at the shared learning rate, where its rows barely move from initialization; trained ten times faster it reaches the level of the other three, with a margin over the baseline that three seeds do not establish. We also document a silent failure: conditioning injected through \texttt{inputs\_embeds} makes Qwen2-VL fall back to one-dimensional positions for its image tokens, which cost nine points.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A GUI agent maps a screenshot and an instruction to a low-level action: an action type such as \emph{click}, \emph{scroll}, or \emph{type}, and, for spatial actions, a target location. Native agent models (UI-TARS~\citep{uitars}, OS-Atlas~\citep{osatlas}, SeeClick~\citep{seeclick}, ShowUI~\citep{showui}) produce both in a single autoregressive stream. The two decisions are different kinds of prediction: the type is a small classification problem, and the location is a localization problem that has to find the evidence on the screen and commit to a point. Several agents therefore predict the type first and the target conditioned on it~\citep{cocoagent,limac,hiagent}, and CoCo-Agent ablates its conditional prediction. What is missing is a comparison of the ways of delivering the type under matched data and compute, and a test of whether the trained model reads the signal at all.

This paper is that comparison, in a small model. We fine-tune Qwen2-VL-2B~\citep{qwen2vl} with LoRA~\citep{lora} to emit a coordinate string and compare a flat baseline with five conditioning mechanisms that see the same data in the same order under the same optimizer, adapters, and decoding. We started from the hypothesis that the prepended token would be the right mechanism, because it gives the model a dedicated, attendable position for the type.

The ranking is clear and our hypothesis was wrong. On a stream that mixes clicks, scrolls, and type events, the auxiliary loss, the additive embedding, and the prompt word raise \hitat{0.10} by five to seven points over the baseline (five seeds, episode-clustered intervals, seed-level $p \le 0.03$), almost entirely on clicks; hard routing and the prepended token are within noise of it. The gain comes from a preprocessing choice of ours: AITW records type events with an off-screen touch point that our serializer clamps to the origin, so the flat model must predict a fixed point for a class it is not told about. Exact emissions of that point explain little of the gap; the class pulls the baseline's clicks toward the origin more broadly, retraining without it raises the baseline by four points, after which no mechanism is established to beat it, and on a separate stream of taps and swipes none helps. With a Stage-1 classifier at 84\% accuracy the pipeline's margin over the flat baseline is not established, and a wrong type collapses every model conditioned at inference.

Interventions on the trained models separate training effects from inference effects: a wrong or blended type costs both embedding models most of their accuracy, so both read their embedding, and the zeroed additive model is no more than a point below the flat baseline whereas the zeroed prepended-token model is seven points below it. The prepended token's learned rows barely move from their random initialization under the shared learning rate; trained ten times faster they move, and the token reaches the level of the other three mechanisms, so its failure is a statement about the schedule, not the architecture. An earlier implementation that injected the conditioning through \texttt{inputs\_embeds} lost nine points to a silent positional-encoding fallback that we first read as evidence against conditioning.

Our contributions are (1) a matched-compute comparison of five ways of delivering the action type, with five seeds, paired statistics at the episode and seed levels, and a control stream; (2) a diagnosis of where the mixed-stream gain comes from, including retraining with the degenerate class removed; (3) a wrong, zero, and class-mean intervention protocol that tests whether a conditioning signal is read and what remains when it is removed; and (4) an account, checked against the library source, of a positional-encoding failure that inverted a conclusion.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{GUI grounding and agent models.}
Mind2Web~\citep{mind2web} and Android in the Wild~\citep{aitw} supply our episodes; AndroidControl~\citep{androidcontrol} and AndroidWorld~\citep{androidworld} extend them. SeeClick~\citep{seeclick} introduced ScreenSpot (extended by ScreenSpot-Pro~\citep{screenspotpro}), and UGround~\citep{uground} and OS-Atlas~\citep{osatlas} scale grounding pretraining. Aguvis~\citep{aguvis}, UI-TARS~\citep{uitars}, CogAgent~\citep{cogagent}, ScreenAI~\citep{screenai}, and Ferret-UI~\citep{ferretui} emit action and location together, the design our flat baseline follows; TinyClick~\citep{tinyclick} and Ferret-UI Lite~\citep{ferretuilite} work at the small scale we study. GUI-Actor~\citep{guiactor} replaces coordinate text with an attention head, MolmoPoint~\citep{molmopoint} and GUI-AIMA~\citep{guiaima} add grounding or anchor tokens, UI-R1~\citep{uir1} rewards type and argument separately, and OmniParser~\citep{omniparser} and Set-of-Mark~\citep{som} externalize grounding into a parser. \citet{imrope} argue that the implicit mapping from rotary position to coordinate limits grounding and add explicit coordinate markers with an interleaved M-RoPE; Section~\ref{sec:mrope} shows a cruder way the same positions go wrong.

\paragraph{Type-first agents.}
CoCo-Agent~\citep{cocoagent} predicts the type and then the target conditioned on it in one stream, our hard-routing variant. LiMAC~\citep{limac} places a small action transformer ahead of a LoRA-tuned Qwen2-VL-2B on AITW and AndroidControl; the action transformer predicts the type and selects click targets contrastively over UI-element embeddings, and the VLM generates only text arguments. AutoGLM~\citep{autoglm} and Hi-Agent~\citep{hiagent} separate planning or subgoals from low-level action, and SeeAct~\citep{seeact}, Ponder\,\&\,Press~\citep{ponderpress}, and Aria-UI~\citep{ariaui} separate text planning from pixel grounding. None holds data, compute, and decoding fixed across ways of delivering the type, or intervenes on the trained model to ask whether the signal is used.

\paragraph{Conditioning, faithfulness, and the backbone.}
SayCan~\citep{saycan}, ReAct~\citep{react}, and RT-2~\citep{rt2} factor, expose, or tokenize the action decision; AutoUI~\citep{autoui}, AppAgent~\citep{appagent}, DigiRL~\citep{digirl}, WebRL~\citep{webrl}, VSC-RL~\citep{vscrl}, and GUI-Libra~\citep{guilibra} improve mobile agents without changing how the type enters grounding; our auxiliary loss is multi-task learning in the sense of \citet{caruana}. Kosmos-2~\citep{kosmos2}, Shikra~\citep{shikra}, and Ferret~\citep{ferret} teach VLMs to refer to regions; POPE~\citep{pope} and HallusionBench~\citep{hallusionbench} ask whether an output is supported by the image, our attention measurements ask that of a coordinate, and \citet{prefill} report that a VLM's grounding decision is largely fixed during prefill. Qwen2-VL~\citep{qwen2vl} assigns three-dimensional rotary positions (extending RoPE~\citep{roformer}) to visual tokens from \texttt{input\_ids}; Section~\ref{sec:mrope} describes what happens when conditioning bypasses that path.

\subsection{Action taxonomy and grounding target}
Both datasets are mapped onto a canonical eight-class action space (\emph{click}, \emph{double-click}, \emph{type}, \emph{scroll}, \emph{drag}, \emph{hotkey}, \emph{wait}, \emph{finished}). Mind2Web's CLICK, SELECT, and HOVER map to \emph{click} and TYPE to \emph{type}. AITW stores gestures as touch and lift points; we split them into taps (\emph{click}) and swipes (\emph{scroll}) by the normalized distance between the two, with threshold 0.04. The grounding target is the touch point normalized to $[0,1]^2$, serialized as \texttt{(x, y)} with integers on a $0$--$999$ grid so every target has the same token length. AITW records non-touch actions such as typing with the touch point $(-1,-1)$; the serializer clamps this to \texttt{(0, 0)}, so every \emph{type} event in the training stream has the same target at the screen origin. Its distance from the raw target is at least $\sqrt{2}$ for any on-screen prediction, so no model can score a hit on a type event; we keep these events in the validation slices, where every model scores zero on them, and Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis} measures what they do to training.

\subsection{Stage 1: which action}
Stage~1 runs the frozen Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct backbone over the screenshot and instruction, mean-pools the final hidden states over unmasked tokens into a 1536-dimensional vector, and trains a three-layer MLP ($1536 \to 1024 \to 256 \to 8$, dropout 0.1) with class-weighted cross-entropy (AdamW, learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01, batch size 128, eight epochs, best validation macro-F1 checkpoint). Features come from text only, text plus screenshot, or a zeroed sanity variant.

\subsection{Stage 2: where}
Stage~2 fine-tunes the same backbone with LoRA adapters (rank 16, $\alpha=32$, dropout 0.05) on the query, key, value, and output projections, 4.4M trainable parameters out of 2.2B. The prompt is the screenshot, \texttt{Goal: \{goal\}}, and a fixed instruction to predict the action coordinate; the answer is the coordinate string. With $s$ the screenshot, $g$ the goal, $a$ the action type, and $y_{1:T}$ the coordinate tokens, every variant minimizes $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coord}} = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, s, g, c)$, masked to the answer tokens, with $c$ the variant-specific conditioning.

\textbf{A, flat.} $c=\varnothing$: the joint-decoding design of native agent models, reduced to the grounding sub-problem.

\textbf{B, auxiliary loss.} A linear head $h_\phi$ on the mean-pooled last hidden state $\bar z$ predicts the action type during training, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coord}} + \lambda\,\mathrm{CE}(h_\phi(\bar z), a)$ with $\lambda=1$. The mean runs over every unmasked position of the teacher-forced sequence, answer tokens included, so the head can read the type off the answer (a type event's answer is \texttt{(0, 0)}). Nothing about the type is provided at inference, so any gain is a training effect.

\textbf{C, hard routing.} The model is trained to emit the action word before the coordinate, \texttt{click (x, y)}, as in CoCo-Agent's conditional action prediction. At test time the word for the gold (or Stage-1) type is forced as a decoding prefix.

\textbf{D-hook, additive embedding.} A learned table $E \in \mathbb{R}^{8\times d}$ holds one vector per class. A forward hook on the text-embedding module adds $E[a]$ to the embedding of every position in \texttt{input\_ids}; Qwen2-VL then overwrites the image-placeholder positions with the visual features, so the bias reaches the text tokens (the prompt and, in training, the answer) and not the image tokens. $E$ is zero-initialized, so D-hook starts as an exact copy of A.

\textbf{D-token, prepended action token.} A new token \texttt{<|action\_slot|>} is added to the vocabulary and placed at the start of the text prompt, so it occupies a real position in \texttt{input\_ids}; a forward hook replaces its embedding with $E[a]$, initialized from $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$. This is the architecture we originally hypothesized: a full-norm, attendable representation of the type. The table shares the base learning rate; Section~\ref{sec:headline} also trains it ten and a hundred times faster.

\textbf{D-text, type as a word.} The prompt begins with \texttt{Action: click.} (or the gold type's word), using the pretrained word embeddings and no new parameters: the obvious baseline for the two learned-embedding variants.

For C, D-hook, D-token, and D-text the gold type is used in training and at evaluation unless stated otherwise; Section~\ref{sec:e2e} closes the loop with Stage-1 predictions. All variants share the data order, the optimizer (AdamW, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$, weight decay 0.01, batch size 1, gradient clipping at 1.0, two epochs), and greedy decoding with 16 new tokens. No hyperparameter was tuned per variant.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.}
Stage~1 uses 7,775 Multimodal-Mind2Web training steps evaluated on the 1,338-step \texttt{test\_task} split, and 5,000 AITW steps from the General subset evaluated on 1,000 held-out steps. Stage~2 uses two AITW streams built by filtering the training split of the \texttt{cjfcsjt/AITW\_General} mirror in its stored order. \mix{all\_with\_coords} keeps taps, swipes, and type events; at 1,200 training examples its validation slice of 250 steps holds 169 clicks, 46 scrolls, and 35 type events from 42 episodes. \mix{taps\_and\_swipes} keeps only taps and swipes (1,000 training and 200 validation steps from 37 episodes), which land anywhere on the screen. For a given mix and size every seed and variant is evaluated on the same examples in the same order, which makes paired tests possible. The validation slice begins where the training slice ends, so absolute numbers are not comparable across training sizes. This is not the official episode split and no action history is given, which limits comparability with published numbers, not the matched comparison. A second control uses Multimodal-Mind2Web (1,000 training and 250 evaluation steps; the target is the gold element's center). Stage~2 is studied on AITW because there the type is a vision problem: Stage~1 gains $+0.414$ macro-F1 from the screenshot on AITW and $+0.009$ on Mind2Web, whose instructions name the action (Table~\ref{tab:stage1}, appendix).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\hitat{r} is the fraction of predictions within normalized Euclidean distance $r$ of the target, for $r \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.25\}$ and, because it is the tap threshold of AITW's action matcher~\citep{aitw}, $r = 0.14$; \hitat{0.10} is the headline, and we report the mean normalized distance. Unparseable outputs count as misses at distance $\sqrt{2}$; only hard routing produces them, and Section~\ref{sec:headline} reports its numbers on parsed outputs as well.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
The headline setting uses five seeds (42 to 46); every other cell uses three (42 to 44). We report the mean and sample standard deviation over seeds. Every run logs a per-example distance on a shared evaluation set with a shared per-seed data order, so comparisons are paired at two levels. Steps of one episode share app, goal, and near-identical screens, so our primary interval is a cluster bootstrap over episodes, which resamples episodes with replacement keeping every step and seed of a sampled episode together (10,000 resamples, 95\% percentile interval, two-sided bootstrap $p$~\citep{efron}). One episode straddles the training/validation boundary in every \mix{all\_with\_coords} slice; dropping its steps changes no estimate by more than 0.003 (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). A paired $t$-test on the five (or three) per-seed means has little power but cannot be fooled by within-seed correlation; we call a difference established only when both agree, and stars come from the episode bootstrap with the seed-level $p$ beside them. No correction is made for the more than fifty contrasts reported; the scaling study (Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}) is descriptive for that reason.

\subsection{Where the gain comes from: a class our serializer made degenerate}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

Table~\ref{tab:perclass} shows where the gain lives. It is a click gain: B and D-text gain nine to ten points on clicks, D-hook six, and C ten while paying sixteen on scrolls. Forcing the word \emph{scroll} derails the gesture: hard routing fails to parse 14\% of its scroll outputs against 1\% of its click outputs, and on parsed outputs its scroll rate is still 0.172, so most of that deficit is misplacement rather than format failure. D-hook and D-text improve both classes. The gain comes from our own preprocessing. The serializer clamps AITW's $(-1,-1)$ touch point for type events to \texttt{(0, 0)}, so the mixed stream contains a class with a fixed target that the flat model must predict with no indication of the type, a situation no deployed action format creates (native formats emit no coordinate for a type action, or emit the type first). The simplest story, that the baseline emits the clamped target for clicks, accounts for little: it does so for 4.3\% of clicks (13\% on its worst seed), B for 2.4\%, and the models conditioned at inference never, and excluding those predictions moves the baseline's click rate only from 0.256 to 0.266. The damage is broader. On the one seed for which we logged predictions, the baseline's click predictions lie 0.10 of the screen width to the left of their targets on average (0.15 among its misses) and 7\% of them fall within 0.10 of the origin, against $-0.02$ and 0.6\% for D-hook on the same screens: the class pulls the baseline's clicks toward the clamped point without making it emit that point exactly.

Retraining measures how much of the gain is protection from that class: we retrain all six variants on the same training slice with the type events removed and score the identical 250 validation examples (three seeds; Table~\ref{tab:notype}, appendix). Removing the class raises the flat baseline from 0.255 to 0.297 \hitat{0.10} ($[+0.015, +0.069]$, seed $p = 0.28$; on clicks from 0.294 to 0.365), leaves B, D-hook, C, and D-token where they were (each changes by less than 0.015, intervals through zero), and costs D-text two points ($-0.024$, $[-0.054, +0.005]$; five at \hitat{0.25}, $[-0.079, -0.028]$). On that stream no mechanism is established to beat the baseline: B's overall margin is $+0.021$ ($[0.000, +0.043]$), D-hook's $+0.008$ ($[-0.018, +0.035]$), D-text's $-0.015$ ($[-0.064, +0.036]$), C's $-0.028$ ($[-0.071, +0.013]$), and D-token's $-0.024$ ($[-0.040, -0.008]$, seed $p = 0.40$), so the data bound any remaining overall advantage at about four points. B keeps a click margin ($+0.039$, $[+0.010, +0.070]$) and a \hitat{0.25} margin ($+0.053$, $[+0.030, +0.076]$) (seed $p = 0.44$ and $0.18$), and D-text's click margin turns negative ($-0.047$, $[-0.082, -0.012]$). With the control in the next section, we read this as follows: on the mixed stream, type supervision protects click grounding from a class that our serialization made degenerate, and we do not claim a spatial prior.

On \mix{taps\_and\_swipes} (Table~\ref{tab:control}), whose training stream has no degenerate class, B and D-hook are within noise of the baseline ($-0.013$ and $-0.018$ \hitat{0.10}, intervals $[-0.042, +0.013]$ and $[-0.056, +0.019]$), so the data admit harms of up to five points as well as no effect; D-text is worse by $-0.033$ ($[-0.063, -0.004]$, seed $p = 0.13$), a harm the bootstrap supports and the seed test does not. \subsection{End to end with predicted types: the deployment number}
\label{sec:e2e}
To close the loop we train D-hook, fit the Stage-1 MLP on frozen features of its training examples (200 full-batch epochs, no checkpoint selection), and evaluate D-hook on the same validation examples with gold and with predicted types (three seeds). Stage~1 reaches 0.843 accuracy against a majority class of 0.676. The oracle pipeline scores 0.292 \hitat{0.10}, the predicted-type pipeline 0.271, and the flat baseline on the same seeds 0.255. The predicted pipeline's margin over the baseline is not established ($+0.016$, $[-0.017, +0.052]$, seed $p = 0.44$); the oracle-to-predicted gap is ($+0.021$, $[+0.003, +0.041]$, seed $p = 0.07$). Classifier error removes more than half of the oracle gain, and Section~\ref{sec:interventions} shows why the classifier is the risk: a wrong type costs the conditioned model far more than a missing one.


\section{Mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Interventions: which signals are read, and what remains when they are removed}
\label{sec:interventions}

\input{tables/interv}

D-token might ignore its slot, and D-hook's gain might be a training effect that leaves a vector the model never reads. To test both on equal terms we retrain each at the headline setting (shared learning rate, five seeds) and decode every trained model under five conditionings on the full validation slice: the gold type; a wrong type from a cyclic permutation of the classes present (click to type, type to scroll, scroll to click); a second wrong map that swaps only clicks and scrolls, so no groundable example is routed to the degenerate class; the table zeroed; and every row replaced by the mean of the rows of the classes present. Table~\ref{tab:interv} reports the results with the flat baseline on the same examples (an earlier three-condition test, Table~\ref{tab:causal}, agrees). D-hook's learned rows are small (norm 0.04 against a mean token-embedding norm of 0.58; D-token's have norm 0.78), and the model reads them. A wrong type collapses D-hook from 0.275 to 0.054 under the cyclic map and to 0.099 under the click-scroll map (gold minus wrong: $+0.222$, $[+0.182, +0.263]$, and $+0.176$, $[+0.134, +0.221]$; seed $p < 0.001$ and $0.003$), so the collapse is not an artifact of routing clicks to the degenerate class. The class-mean vector costs seven points ($+0.070$, $[+0.034, +0.109]$), though the seed-level test does not reach significance ($p = 0.13$). Zeroing the table costs $+0.027$ with an interval from $-0.006$ to $+0.065$ ($p = 0.22$): the data are consistent with no cost and with a cost of six points, most of the $+0.046$ ($[+0.016, +0.081]$) that gold conditioning gives over the baseline on these examples. What can be stated is a bound: the zeroed additive model is no more than one point below the flat baseline (zeroed minus A: $+0.019$, $[-0.006, +0.044]$). A single-seed attention run that we had first read as showing the embedding could be removed without loss (Appendix~\ref{app:attention}) was underpowered.

D-token differs in two respects. Every perturbation lowers its accuracy and the bootstrap intervals exclude zero for all four, including the class-mean condition ($+0.073$, $[+0.046, +0.100]$), but with five seeds only the cyclic wrong type also passes the seed-level test ($p = 0.001$; click-scroll map $0.07$, zero $0.12$, class mean $0.09$). Its zeroed model is below the flat baseline by $-0.067$ ($[-0.089, -0.044]$, seed $p < 0.01$), whereas the zeroed D-hook is not: adaptation with a prepended token makes the model depend on the token, adaptation with an additive vector does not, even though the vector is used. The prompt word behaves like the prepended token (Table~\ref{tab:interv_dtext}, appendix; three seeds): a wrong word collapses D-text from 0.312 to 0.076 and 0.085 under the two maps (gold minus wrong $+0.236$, $[+0.183, +0.289]$, and $+0.227$, $[+0.174, +0.281]$; seed $p = 0.017$ and $0.014$), and with no word at all it scores 0.205, below the flat baseline by $-0.049$ ($[-0.079, -0.018]$, seed $p = 0.44$). Under a wrong type, the failure a deployed classifier produces, the additive model is the most brittle: 0.054 and 0.099 against D-token's 0.079 and 0.155 and D-text's 0.076 and 0.085. In every mechanism the conditioning acts almost entirely on clicks; attention at the y-predicting token moves with accuracy under these interventions on one seed (Appendix~\ref{app:attention}).

\subsection{A positional-encoding failure that inverted a conclusion}
\label{sec:mrope}
Our first implementation of the prepended token replaced the slot's embedding by building \texttt{inputs\_embeds} and passing that to the model instead of \texttt{input\_ids}. On \mix{taps\_and\_swipes} that variant scored $0.298 \pm 0.026$ \hitat{0.10} against $0.390 \pm 0.010$ for the flat baseline in the same set of runs, and a variant with the same slot but a frozen random embedding scored the same (0.290), which pointed at the injection path. Qwen2-VL derives its three-dimensional rotary positions from \texttt{input\_ids}; given \texttt{inputs\_embeds} alone, transformers falls back to one-dimensional positions replicated across the three axes, and the image tokens lose their two-dimensional layout (Appendix~\ref{app:mrope}; \citealp{hfissue35463}). Modifying embeddings through a forward hook with \texttt{input\_ids} intact removed the deficit. We inferred the cause from the library source and the frozen-embedding control rather than from position ids dumped in the failing runs; we record it because the failure is silent.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The study is small: one 2B backbone, LoRA adapters, validation slices of 200 to 250 steps from 35 to 50 episodes, one learning rate and adapter configuration for every variant. The absolute numbers are far below the field's leaderboards, the split is a stored-order slice rather than the official episode split, no action history is given, and per-seed variance is large, so the conclusions rest on paired statistics at two levels, neither clean: the episode bootstrap ignores that seeds share a data order, and the seed-level test has five or three units. The prepended token's failure at the shared learning rate is a schedule effect, and the faster-trained token has three seeds; we did not train a frozen-slot control on the fixed path, and the class-mean intervention is matched to D-token in direction but not in norm. The positive result is about adaptation on a stream containing a class our serializer made degenerate, and the clean-stream result bounds rather than excludes a small advantage. The end-to-end result is descriptive, the scaling study has 24 uncorrected contrasts on moving slices, the attention analysis uses one seed and one layer with a probe token chosen after the fact, the retracted result was compared with the fixed additive variant rather than the same variant on both paths, and Mind2Web is at floor. Code, split indices, prompts, and per-example logs will be released with the camera-ready.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
An auxiliary loss, an additive embedding, and a word in the prompt each help a small grounding model on a training stream that our serialization gave a degenerate class, and none is established to help once that class is removed or on taps and swipes, and the prepended token we set out to validate helps only when its table is trained faster than the shared schedule, and then no better than the simpler mechanisms. Both learned embeddings are read at inference: zeroed, the additive model stays within a point of the baseline and the prepended one falls seven points below it, and a wrong type collapses both, so the classifier that supplies the type is the risk: with an 84\% classifier the pipeline's margin over a flat model is not established. Check what a serialization does to every class before supervising a coordinate on it, prefer the prompt word or the auxiliary loss over new parameters, perturb the conditioning to test it, and keep \texttt{input\_ids} on the path.

\section{Training and evaluation details}
\label{app:details}
\paragraph{Data.} AITW screenshots in the \texttt{cjfcsjt/AITW\_General} mirror are stored as raw RGB byte buffers without an image header; we decode them by matching the buffer length against seven observed resolutions (for example $540\times1140$ and $412\times732$) with a portrait-aspect fallback. Mind2Web screenshots are downscaled so that no image exceeds one megapixel. Examples are drawn from the AITW training stream in stored order, filtered to the mix's action labels; the first $n$ eligible steps form the training slice and the next 250 (200 for the control mix) form validation. Consecutive steps belong to the same episodes, so a validation slice spans a few dozen episodes (35 to 50 across the sizes we use) and shares its boundary episode with training (two to ten steps, four at the headline size). Table~\ref{tab:scaling} lists the class mix of each validation slice.

\paragraph{Prompts.} Every Stage-2 prompt is the chat-template user turn containing the screenshot, the line \texttt{Goal: \{goal\}}, and the instruction \texttt{Predict the action coordinate.}; the assistant turn is the answer \texttt{(x, y)} with integers on the $0$--$999$ grid. C uses \texttt{Predict the action type and coordinate.} and is trained to answer with the action word before the coordinate, \texttt{click (x, y)}; D-text prefixes the user turn with \texttt{Action: \{word\}.}; D-token places \texttt{<|action\_slot|>} at the start of the text, and exactly one slot per sequence is asserted at every step.

\paragraph{Optimization.} AdamW with learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ and weight decay 0.01, batch size 1, gradient clipping at 1.0, two epochs, a fixed per-epoch shuffle seeded by the run seed. The action-embedding table (D-hook, D-token) and the auxiliary head (B) share the base learning rate unless stated otherwise. Evaluation is greedy with at most 16 new tokens; the coordinate is parsed with a regular expression and an unparseable output counts as a miss at distance $\sqrt{2}$. Greedy evaluation is deterministic for a trained model; models in different tables that share a seed are separately trained copies (Table~\ref{tab:causal} and the intervention runs retrain the variant), and their numbers differ by training nondeterminism.

\paragraph{Compute.} Everything runs on single NVIDIA L4 GPUs. A Stage-2 run at 1,200 training examples takes about 35 minutes; the full study, including retracted and re-run cells, cost about 150 GPU-hours.

\paragraph{Statistics.} For each contrast we align the per-example distances of the two variants on every shared seed. The episode bootstrap resamples the episodes of the validation slice with replacement, keeps every step and every seed of a sampled episode together, recomputes the mean difference 10,000 times, and reports the percentile interval and the two-sided bootstrap $p$. The seed-level test is a paired $t$-test on the per-seed means. Clustering on examples instead of episodes would add one star in the paper (hard routing's headline gain, example-cluster interval $[+0.001, +0.069]$); the (seed, example)-unit bootstrap and its permutation counterpart give intervals about a third narrower still and are reported in the released logs. Every star in this paper was recomputed on the episode basis.

\section{The \texttt{inputs\_embeds} failure in detail}
\label{app:mrope}
Qwen2-VL locates the image tokens in \texttt{input\_ids} to assign each visual token a temporal, height, and width position for its three-dimensional rotary embedding. When a caller supplies \texttt{inputs\_embeds} and no \texttt{input\_ids}, the model cannot perform that lookup; in the transformers releases our runs used (pinned at 4.45 or later; the current 5.9 release behaves the same) the positions become a one-dimensional sequence index replicated across the three axes, so every image token is positioned as text and the two-dimensional layout the grounding task depends on is lost. A caller on that path must also merge the visual features into the embeddings itself, a second opportunity for silent error. We inferred the cause from the code and from the frozen-embedding control described in Section~\ref{sec:mrope} rather than by dumping position ids at the time. The same dependence on \texttt{input\_ids} was reported as a generation crash in December 2024~\citep{hfissue35463}; the silent degradation when images are present is the case a fine-tuning practitioner meets. In the set of runs where the failure appeared, the fixed additive variant scored $0.392 \pm 0.043$ \hitat{0.10} on \mix{taps\_and\_swipes} against the flat baseline's $0.390 \pm 0.010$; the re-run of that control with per-example logging (Table~\ref{tab:control}) gives $0.363 \pm 0.021$ against $0.382 \pm 0.015$.

We measured where the models look, with the caveats that the measure is head-averaged attention at one layer, that attention is not information flow, and that teacher-forcing the gold answer places the gold x coordinate in the prefix of the token that predicts y. Under gold conditioning both embedding models put 10 to 11\% of that token's image attention within $0.10$ of the target, against a chance share of 2.6\%; the last prompt token, which our earlier visualizations used, puts about 4\% there under every conditioning, so most of the localization we can see happens after the x coordinate is in the prefix. Teacher-forcing the model's own answer instead of the gold one, so that the y-predicting token sees the predicted x, lowers the target share only slightly (D-hook 0.092, D-token 0.085), so the localization is not an artifact of the supplied x.
