%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT
\begin{abstract}
GUI agents must predict an action type (\emph{click}, \emph{scroll},
\emph{type}, \ldots) and a spatial target from a screenshot and an
instruction. We study whether explicitly factoring these decisions helps a
2B-parameter agent, via a matched-compute comparison of five conditioning
mechanisms on Qwen2-VL: a flat baseline (A), an auxiliary action-type loss
(B), hard routing (C), an additive embedding hook (D-hook), and the
prepended learned action embedding our original proposal advocated
(D-token). Our first evaluation of this question was wrong in three
independent ways, each of which we diagnose, quantify, and repair:
(i) injecting conditioning via \texttt{inputs\_embeds} silently bypasses
Qwen2-VL's M-RoPE position path and manufactured an $8\sigma$ \emph{false
negative}; (ii) train/validation splits taken as consecutive slices of one
episode stream leaked shared task goals (\LeakGoalMain{} of validation
steps at the headline setting, up to \LeakGoalMax{} at scale) while looking
clean at the episode level (\LeakEpMain{}); and (iii) a paired bootstrap
pooling (seed, example) predictions as independent units was
anti-conservative, rejecting a true clustered null \TypeIPooled{} of the
time at nominal 5\% (an episode-clustered replacement achieves
\TypeIClustered{}). We then rebuilt the data pipeline around frozen
goal-disjoint splits with a test region no phase of the project had ever
touched, prespecified four contrasts, and evaluated once. \textbf{Every
mechanistic conclusion of the original study reversed}, and the statistical
repair alone would not have caught most of them. The auxiliary loss that the repaired
statistics still endorsed on historical data (B$-$A \ExpBvsA{},
$p=\ExpBvsAp$, versus the invalid pooled $p=\ExpBvsApooled$) is null on the
untouched test set (\ConfBvsA{}, Holm $p=\ConfBvsAHolm$); the prepended
action embedding our proposal advocated and the course-era version reported
as \emph{refuted} in fact beats it (D-token$-$B \ConfDtvsB{}, Holm
$p=\ConfDtvsBHolm$) --- though D-token reads the ground-truth action type at
test time and B reads nothing, so that contrast is oracle-versus-deployable;
the largest \emph{relative} gain moves to \emph{scroll}
($\ConfScrollFold\times$), while click still supplies \ConfClickAbsShare{} of
the absolute gain; the control condition designed to show no
effect shows one, and is therefore invalid; and the deployable
predicted-type pipeline is null (\ConfEtoEvsA{}, Holm
$p=\ConfEtoEvsAHolm$) because Stage~1 accuracy falls from
\ConfStageOneValAcc{} on the frozen validation split to \ConfStageOneAcc{}
on the untouched test region, absorbing \ConfEtoEGapShare{} of the oracle
gain and leaving no detectable benefit. What survives is narrow and honest:
\emph{oracle} action-type conditioning improves grounding
(D-hook$-$A \ConfDhvsA{}, Holm $p=\ConfDhvsAHolm$), and nothing we can
actually deploy does. We release the frozen splits, per-example
predictions, and the clustered-inference code. A study can be multi-seed,
ablated, controlled, and apparently significant at the pooled
$p{=}\ExpBvsApooled{}$ we now retract, and still have every one of its
mechanistic claims move under a clean split and a calibrated test.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A GUI agent maps a screenshot and a natural-language instruction to a
low-level action: a type (\emph{click}, \emph{scroll}, \emph{type},
\emph{drag}, \ldots) and, for spatial actions, a pixel-level target.
State-of-the-art native agents (UI-TARS~\cite{uitars},
OS-Atlas~\cite{osatlas}, SeeClick~\cite{seeclick}, ShowUI~\cite{showui})
emit both in one autoregressive stream, entangling the two decisions. A
documented failure mode follows: commit to the wrong action type, then
ground \emph{coherently} within the wrong semantic frame -- scrolling past a
visible target instead of clicking it. This motivated our original
hypothesis, formulated as an architecture bet: a learned action-type
embedding, prepended to the instruction, should let a grounding model
condition on \emph{what} before deciding \emph{where}, and should help most
in low data.

This paper reports what happened when we tested that bet carefully, and it
has two intertwined subjects. The first is the scientific question:
\emph{when does action-type supervision change GUI grounding, and through
which mechanism?} The second is methodological: \emph{which implementation
and evaluation choices can create a false appearance of benefit or harm in
studies of exactly this kind?} We came to the second subject involuntarily
-- three separate artifacts in our own pipeline each produced a wrong
conclusion at some point during the project -- and we believe the
post-mortem is at least as useful to practitioners as the object-level
result.

Concretely, we compare five matched-compute Stage-2 conditioning mechanisms
on Qwen2-VL-2B~\cite{qwen2vl} with LoRA, over AITW~\cite{aitw} with a
Mind2Web~\cite{mind2web} cross-dataset control, preceded by a Stage-1
action-type classifier study. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A confirmatory answer, and it is mostly negative.} On an
untouched, goal-disjoint test set: auxiliary action-type supervision does
not measurably help; \emph{oracle} inference-time conditioning does
(\ConfDhvsA{}); the deployable predicted-type pipeline does not, because
the action-type classifier drops from \ConfStageOneValAcc{} to
\ConfStageOneAcc{} accuracy between the two held-out regions; and the
proportionally largest effect moves to \emph{scroll} rather than the click
disambiguation our exploratory analysis reported, though click's higher
frequency still makes it \ConfClickAbsShare{} of the absolute gain.
\item \textbf{Three quantified evaluation pitfalls.} (1) A silent M-RoPE /
\texttt{inputs\_embeds} interaction that fabricated an $8\sigma$ false
negative; (2) episode-stream train/validation leakage that is invisible at
the episode level (\LeakEpMain{} of validation steps) but large at the
shared-task-goal level (\LeakGoalMain--\LeakGoalMax); (3) pseudo-replicated
significance testing whose anti-conservativeness we measure on synthetic
clustered nulls (\TypeIPooled{} type-I error at nominal 5\%).
\item \textbf{A repaired, releasable protocol.} Pinned-revision,
goal-disjoint frozen train/validation/test manifests with an untouched test
region beyond every historical data access; episode-clustered paired
inference with per-seed effects and validated calibration; and a
prespecified confirmatory analysis committed before test access. All
artifacts, per-example predictions, and analysis code are released.
\item \textbf{A measured cost of getting evaluation wrong.} Because we ran
the same ablation twice --- once under the flawed protocol, once under the
repaired one --- we can report what the flaws actually bought: not inflated
$p$-values alone, but a reversal of which mechanism wins, which action class
carries the gain, whether our own hypothesis was refuted, whether the
pipeline is deployable, and whether the control holds
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{End-to-end GUI agents.} The dominant paradigm treats action type,
arguments, and coordinates as one autoregressive output. UI-TARS~\cite{uitars}
exemplifies this with a unified action space across platforms and explicit
thought traces. OS-Atlas~\cite{osatlas} scales the same paradigm with 13M
cross-platform GUI grounding instances and shows that action-label
harmonization across web/Android/desktop is nontrivial. ShowUI~\cite{showui}
builds a compact VLA around Qwen2-VL with interleaved vision-language-action
streaming. Our flat baseline (Variant~A) replicates the joint-decoding
paradigm of these systems.

\textbf{Modular grounding.} SeeClick~\cite{seeclick} argues that grounding
is the central bottleneck in visual GUI agents. UGround /
SeeAct-V~\cite{uground} separates planning from grounding at the
text-vs-pixel boundary. GUI-Actor~\cite{guiactor} replaces coordinate-token
regression with an attention-based action head driven by a dedicated
\texttt{<ACTOR>} token plus a verifier. Our work studies a different module
boundary: between \emph{action-type prediction} and \emph{spatial
grounding}, not between planning text and coordinates.

\textbf{Reward decomposition.} UI-R1~\cite{uir1} applies RL fine-tuning
with a rule-based reward that separates action-type accuracy from
action-argument accuracy on AndroidControl. It is the closest training
analog: type and location as separable optimization targets; we use
supervised conditioning rather than RL. RegionFocus~\cite{regionfocus}
refines grounding at inference; we change the training-time conditioning
pathway. SayCan~\cite{saycan} and ReAct~\cite{react} provide the
modular-decision precedent, and RT-2~\cite{rt2} the unified token-level
action generation paradigm we critically examine.

\textbf{Evaluation integrity.} Our methodological findings echo, in a GUI
setting, long-standing concerns about test-set contamination and
dependence-blind inference: grouped data require grouped splits and
cluster-aware uncertainty, and repeated measurements of the same example
must not be pooled as independent evidence. Mind2Web's own metric design
(element accuracy vs.\ operation F1 vs.\ step success)~\cite{mind2web}
reflects the type/location decomposition we study.

\subsection{Two evaluation protocols}
\label{sec:protocols}

\textbf{Exploratory protocol (Phases 1--7, historical).} Our original runs
streamed the AITW training split in mirror order, filtered by data mix, and
took \emph{consecutive slices}: the first $n_{\text{train}}$ steps for
training and the next $n_{\text{val}}$ for validation. This is the kind of
split that looks innocuous in code review and is not: episodes and,
worse, repeated task goals straddle the boundary
(Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). Every historical number in this paper is
labeled exploratory and is re-analyzed with dependence-aware statistics.

\textbf{Confirmatory protocol (Phase 9, frozen).} We rebuilt the data
pipeline around a frozen manifest: the first 12{,}000
\texttt{all\_with\_coords} steps of the pinned mirror revision, grouped by
\emph{normalized task goal} (episodes never span goals), split
goal-disjointly into train (9{,}511 steps), validation (1{,}660), and an
\textbf{untouched test region} (829): test groups are drawn exclusively
from beyond stream index 5{,}500, past the furthest index any historical
run ever loaded (5{,}250), so no test step was trained on, evaluated on,
or inspected during the exploratory phase. Evaluation sets are frozen and
identical for every variant and training size (250 validation / 600 test
steps for \texttt{all\_with\_coords}; 200/400 for the control mix);
training subsets are nested prefixes. The manifest, split hashes, 17
disclosed near-duplicate goal pairs (token Jaccard $\geq 0.8$), duplicate
checks, and the prespecified analysis (contrasts, metrics, corrections,
failed-run rules) were committed to version control \emph{before} any
test-set evaluation ran. We claim ``decisions frozen before test access,''
documented by commit history -- not third-party preregistration.

\subsection{Stage 2: five conditioning mechanisms}

Stage~2 fine-tunes Qwen2-VL-2B with LoRA (rank 16 on q/k/v/o projections,
$\approx$0.2\% of parameters) to emit the coordinate string; all variants
share data, optimizer (lr $2\times10^{-5}$, 2 epochs, batch 1), and
evaluation. The only difference is how action-type information enters:

\textbf{A (flat baseline).} No conditioning; replicates joint decoding.

\textbf{B (auxiliary loss).} A linear action-type head on the pooled
representation adds cross-entropy to the grounding loss
($\lambda{=}1$); \emph{no action information at inference}.

\textbf{C (hard routing).} Trained to emit ``\texttt{\{word\} (x, y)}'';
at inference the gold action word is forced as the decode prefix.

\textbf{D-hook (additive embedding).} A learned table
$E\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times d}$; the row for the current action type is added
to \emph{all} token embeddings via a forward hook, preserving the normal
\texttt{input\_ids} path (embedding norm at convergence $\approx0.07$ at
the headline setting).

\textbf{D-token (prepended token -- the original hypothesis).} A sentinel
\texttt{<|action\_slot|>} token is prepended to the instruction and its
embedding replaced in place by $E[a]$, M-RoPE-correctly (the token is in
\texttt{input\_ids}); norm at convergence $\approx2.2$, a strong routable
signal.

\section{Three Evaluation Pitfalls, Quantified}
\label{sec:pitfalls}

\subsection{Pitfall 1: M-RoPE vs.\ \texttt{inputs\_embeds}}
\label{sec:mrope}

Qwen2-VL assigns 3D (temporal, height, width) position IDs to visual
tokens via an \texttt{input\_ids}-keyed path. Our first D-token
implementation injected the action embedding by passing
\texttt{inputs\_embeds} directly, which silently bypasses that path:
image-token positions were computed without the action slot in the
sequence, misaligning multimodal position embeddings for \emph{every}
visual token. The observed effect was a confident $8\sigma$ ``A beats D''
result -- a pure artifact. Rebuilding the same mechanism through the
\texttt{input\_ids} path (D-hook, D-token) erased the deficit. Any
conditioning study on M-RoPE-family models that touches
\texttt{inputs\_embeds} should verify position-ID computation explicitly;
the artifact is silent, large, and directionally misleading.

\subsection{Pitfall 2: stream-slice splits leak task goals}
\label{sec:leakage}

Table~\ref{tab:leakage} quantifies the historical protocol's leakage by
reconstructing, for every historical cell, which episodes and normalized
task goals each validation step shared with that cell's training slice.
Episode-level overlap -- the leakage one usually checks -- is small
(\LeakEpMain{} at the headline cell). The dominant channel is
\emph{shared task goals}: \LeakGoalMain{} of headline validation steps
have a goal string that also appears in training, rising to \LeakGoalMax{}
at $n_{\text{train}}{=}5000$ -- AITW goals repeat across many episodes, so
consecutive-slice splitting almost guarantees template overlap at scale.
Val-set difficulty therefore varies with $n_{\text{train}}$ (each size had
a different val slice), quietly confounding the historical scaling curve.
The confirmatory protocol removes both channels by construction
(goal-disjoint splits; Section~\ref{sec:protocols}).

\subsection{Pitfall 3: pseudo-replicated significance}
\label{sec:stats}

Our original headline significance pooled per-example distances across
seeds -- treating $3\times250$ (seed, example) rows as 750 independent
paired observations. Predictions of the same example by different seeds
are strongly correlated, and examples within an episode share state; the
pooled bootstrap therefore overstates evidence. On synthetic nulls
matching the data's dependence structure (episode and example effects
shared across seeds; zero true difference), the pooled test rejects
\TypeIPooled{} of the time at nominal 5\% (\TypeISims{} simulations),
while the replacement below achieves \TypeIClustered{}.

\textbf{Repaired inference.} For each contrast we (i) average each
example's paired difference over seeds, (ii) cluster examples by episode,
(iii) bootstrap clusters (10{,}000 resamples) for 95\% CIs, and (iv) use
an episode-level sign-flip permutation test (10{,}000 permutations;
$p$-values reported no smaller than Monte Carlo resolution -- never
$p{=}0$). \textbf{Estimand.} This tests the \emph{fixed trained
checkpoints} on new data from the eval population. It is \emph{not}
calibrated for claims about the training procedure: adding run-level
(seed) offsets to the synthetic null drives the episode-clustered test to
\TypeIProc{} rejection (and the pooled test to 70\%). With only three
seeds per variant we therefore
release per-seed means for every contrast (in
\texttt{rerun\_analysis.json} and \texttt{reanalysis.json}) and make no
procedure-level significance claims. The retracted pooled $p$-values (including a headline
$p{=}\ExpBvsApooled$) should not be cited.

\textbf{P1 (auxiliary supervision) --- null.} B vs.\ A on hit@0.10:

\textbf{P2 (inference-time conditioning) --- positive, but oracle.} D-hook
vs.\ A: \ConfDhvsA{} (Holm $p=\ConfDhvsAHolm$; mean L2 \ConfDhvsALtwo{}),
positive in all three seeds (per-seed deltas $+0.035$ to $+0.085$). This is the one large

D-hook receives the ground-truth action type at test time, which A never
sees. P4 is what happens when that oracle is replaced by a real classifier.

\textbf{P3 (the proposal's mechanism) --- reversed in our favour, and we
flag it as such.} D-token vs.\ B: \ConfDtvsB{} (Holm
$p=\ConfDtvsBHolm$) --- the prepended learned action embedding our original
proposal advocated significantly \emph{outperforms} the auxiliary loss on
the clean test set. The exploratory data pointed the other way
(Section~\ref{sec:exploratory}), and the course-era version of this work
reported the embedding as refuted. On the untouched test both embedding
mechanisms beat B (D-token vs.\ B \ConfDtvsB{}; D-hook vs.\ B
\ConfDhvsB{}, $p=\ConfDhvsBp$) and both beat A (D-token vs.\ A
\ConfDtvsA{}, $p=\ConfDtvsAp$), while hard routing is exactly null
(C vs.\ A \ConfCvsA{}, $p=\ConfCvsAp$).

Two cautions on reading P3. First, it is an \emph{oracle-versus-deployable}
comparison, not a like-for-like mechanism ranking: D-token and D-hook read
the ground-truth action type at inference while B reads nothing, so the
embedding variants are being credited with information B never receives.
Second, we neither prespecified nor ran a D-hook versus D-token contrast,
so we make no claim that either embedding route is better than the other.
What P3 does establish is that the course-era verdict --- ``the prepended
embedding is refuted'' --- does not survive a clean split. We stress that
this reverses in the direction that flatters our own hypothesis, which is
precisely why it needed a prespecified test on data we had never touched.

\textbf{P4 (deployable pipeline) --- null.} End-to-end predicted-type
pipeline vs.\ A: \ConfEtoEvsA{} (Holm $p=\ConfEtoEvsAHolm$). The reason is
Stage~1 generalization, not Stage~2: the action-type classifier scores
\ConfStageOneValAcc{} accuracy on the frozen validation split but only
\ConfStageOneAcc{} on the untouched test region, and the paired
predicted-vs-oracle gap is \ConfEtoEGap{} ($p=\ConfEtoEGapp$). That gap
absorbs \ConfEtoEGapShare{} of the oracle arm's advantage over A
($\ConfEtoEOracle$ versus $\ConfAhit$, i.e.\ \ConfEtoEOracleAdv{}), leaving
a residual that is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
\textbf{Stage-1 error leaves no detectable benefit}, directly contradicting
the historical claim that ``classifier error is not the bottleneck.''
We note what this drop is \emph{not}: both the frozen validation split and
the untouched test region are goal-disjoint from training, so novel task
goals alone cannot explain the difference between them. The two held-out
sets differ in where they sit in the episode stream (the test region lies
entirely beyond index 5{,}500), and we did not run the ablations needed to
isolate that cause, so we report the drop as an observed generalization gap
rather than a diagnosed one. Either way it is the binding constraint on the
deployable pipeline.

\subsection{The control condition does not behave as designed}
\label{sec:controlfail}

Our prespecified control assumed that on \texttt{taps\_and\_swipes}
action type carries no spatial information, so conditioning should not
help. It does help: D-hook vs.\ A on the control test set is
\ConfCtrlDhvsA{} ($p=\ConfCtrlDhvsAp$), while B is null
(\ConfCtrlBvsA{}, $p=\ConfCtrlBvsAp$) and C is significantly harmful
(\ConfCtrlCvsA{}, $p=\ConfCtrlCvsAp$). These control contrasts were
prespecified as \emph{secondary}, and their $p$-values are unadjusted; we
use them to \emph{withdraw} a scope claim rather than to establish one,
which is the direction in which an unadjusted secondary result can safely
be read. We report this as a \textbf{failed
control}, not a second confirmation. It is coherent with
Section~\ref{sec:confperclass}: if the exploitable signal is ``swipes land
in a predictable region,'' then a tap-versus-swipe mix is exactly a
setting where action type \emph{is} spatially informative, and our control
was mis-designed rather than uninformative. The consequence for the paper
is concrete: we cannot claim ``conditioning helps if and only if action
type is spatially predictive,'' because we no longer have a matched
condition in which it demonstrably is not.

\subsection{Low data}

The confirmatory low-data grid (Table~\ref{tab:lowdata}) evaluates every
training size against the \emph{same} frozen test set, removing the
per-size-val-set confound that made Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} unreadable.
D-hook's oracle advantage is present at $n{=}300$ and $n{=}800$ and not
resolved at $n{=}500$; B is positive only at $n{=}300$. There is no clean

\subsection{What the broken evaluation actually cost}
\label{sec:cost}

Because the same five-variant ablation was run under both protocols, we can
separate the two defects and ask what each one cost. Three states are
distinguishable: \textbf{(i)} the original analysis (leaky split, pooled
bootstrap) --- what a course-era version of this paper reported;
\textbf{(ii)} the statistically repaired analysis on the \emph{same} leaky
data (Section~\ref{sec:exploratory}); and \textbf{(iii)} the confirmatory
analysis on the untouched split.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Which variant wins.} (i) B beats A, $p{=}\ExpBvsApooled$.
(ii) B still beats A, \ExpBvsA{}, $p{=}\ExpBvsAp$. (iii) \emph{B is null}
(\ConfBvsA{}); the embedding mechanisms are the only ones that help.
\item \textbf{Whether our own hypothesis was refuted.} (i) D-token is
significantly worse than B (pooled $p{=}\ExpDtvsBpooled$), so the proposal's
prepended embedding is refuted. (ii) the gap is no longer significant
(\ExpDtvsB{}, $p{=}\ExpDtvsBp$) --- the refutation was already an artifact
of the pooled test. (iii) \emph{D-token significantly beats B}
(\ConfDtvsB{}).
\item \textbf{Where the gain lives.} (i)--(ii) click disambiguation.
(iii) the \emph{relative} effect is largest on \emph{scroll}
($\ConfScrollFold\times$ versus $\ConfClickFold\times$ for click), though
click's higher frequency still makes it \ConfClickAbsShare{} of the
absolute gain.
\item \textbf{Whether the pipeline is deployable.} (i) the predicted-type
pipeline was reported as beating A, with ``classifier error is not the
bottleneck'' --- a descriptive comparison that no test ever supported.
(ii) any calibrated test shows it was never significant
(\ExpEtoEPredvsA{}, $p=\ExpEtoEPredvsAp$). (iii) the
pipeline is \emph{null}, with Stage-1 error absorbing \ConfEtoEGapShare{}
of the oracle gain.
\item \textbf{Whether the control holds.} (i)--(ii) conditioning looks
neutral where action type was assumed uninformative. (iii) the control
shows a significant effect, so it is \emph{not a valid control}.
\end{itemize}

Two lessons separate cleanly. First, the statistical repair alone was
\emph{not} sufficient: it withdrew two unsupported claims (the D-token
refutation and the deployable pipeline) but left the headline ``auxiliary
supervision works'' and the click-mechanism story intact and wrong ---
those required the clean split. Fixing inference without fixing the data would have
produced a paper that was better calibrated and still substantively
mistaken. Second, the direction of error is not systematic: the flawed
protocol inflated some effects, suppressed our own hypothesis, and
mis-located the mechanism. A study can be internally consistent,
multi-seed, ablated, controlled, and significant at the pooled
$p{=}\ExpBvsApooled{}$ we now retract, and still have every one of its
mechanistic claims move under a clean split and a calibrated test.

\section{Limitations}

Every positive effect among the four prespecified contrasts is
oracle-gated: D-hook and D-token receive ground-truth action types that a
deployed agent would have to predict, and when we predict them the effect
disappears (P4). No headline claim in this paper should be read as a
deployable improvement. One non-oracle positive does appear among the
secondary results --- B over A at $n_{\text{train}}{=}300$
(Table~\ref{tab:lowdata}) --- but it is a secondary, unadjusted contrast in
a grid where the same comparison is null at both larger sizes, so we do not
build a claim on it. Our control
condition failed (Section~\ref{sec:controlfail}), so we have no valid
matched setting in which action type is demonstrably uninformative; the
scope condition on when conditioning helps is therefore untested, and
designing a genuine control (for instance, targets randomized within class)
is future work. The scroll-versus-click mechanism reading in
Section~\ref{sec:confperclass} is a post-hoc decomposition of confirmatory
data, not a prespecified test.

Offline step-level hit@radius is not long-horizon task success: it scores
logged single steps, without interaction, recovery, or compounding-error
dynamics -- the motivating failure mode is measured here only via its
step-level signature. The backbone is a single 2B model; conclusions are
about this scale and training recipe, not about GUI agents generally, and
our baselines are small matched-compute models trained by us, not
state-of-the-art systems.
The confirmatory estimand is fixed checkpoints, not the training
procedure (three seeds; per-seed effects reported). AITW's \texttt{type}
coordinates are degenerate, so ``grounding'' on
\texttt{all\_with\_coords} is effectively click/scroll grounding plus a
constant; the click-concentration analysis is post-hoc on exploratory
data and only its prespecified aggregate versions are confirmatory. Goal
strings are our dependence proxy; 17 near-duplicate train/test goal pairs
(disclosed in the manifest) survive normalization, and subtler sharing
(same app, similar screens) is not modeled. The Mind2Web control used 2
seeds without per-example logs. Finally, the mirror we pin is a
third-party repackaging of AITW; we verified revision stability but not
byte-level provenance against the original release.

\section{Conclusion}

On a test set whose task goals were never seen in training, the
factorization thesis survives only in oracle form. Telling a 2B GUI agent
the ground-truth action type improves grounding
(\ConfDhvsA{} hit@0.10). Both mechanisms that consume that oracle signal
beat the auxiliary loss, including the prepended action embedding our
proposal advocated (D-token$-$B \ConfDtvsB{}) --- a reversal of the
course-era verdict that the embedding was refuted, though the comparison is
oracle-versus-deployable, and we neither prespecified nor ran a D-hook
versus D-token contrast, so we do not claim either embedding mechanism is
better than the other. But auxiliary supervision alone is null, hard
routing is null, the largest \emph{relative} effect moves to \emph{scroll}
rather than the click disambiguation we previously reported (click still
supplies \ConfClickAbsShare{} of the absolute gain), our control condition
turns out not to be a control, and the only configuration one could
actually deploy --- predicting the action type and conditioning on the
prediction --- is statistically indistinguishable from doing nothing,
because the classifier drops from \ConfStageOneValAcc{} to
\ConfStageOneAcc{} accuracy between the frozen validation split and the
untouched test region. The honest summary is that we measured a real but
oracle-gated effect and no deployable one.

The methodological result is the durable one. We ran the same five-variant
ablation twice: once under a protocol with three defects (an
\texttt{inputs\_embeds}/M-RoPE interaction fabricating an $8\sigma$ false
negative, goal-level split leakage reaching \LeakGoalMax{}, and a pooled
bootstrap with \TypeIPooled{} measured type-I error), and once under a
repaired one. The defects did not merely exaggerate significance; they
inverted which mechanism wins, which class carries the gain, whether our
hypothesis was refuted, whether the pipeline was deployable, and whether
the control held. None of the three is exotic --- stream-sliced splits,
embedding-injection shortcuts, and pooled per-seed bootstraps are ordinary
practice --- so we expect the same reversals are latent in other offline
GUI-grounding evaluations. The frozen splits, per-example predictions,
clustered-inference code, and prespecification are released so that both
the object-level result and the audit can be checked and rerun.
